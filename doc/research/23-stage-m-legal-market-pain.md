## TL;DR

1. **Sanctions law is real and rising.** From the $1.45B Coleman / Morgan Stanley verdict (2005) through the SEC's $1.8B off-channel-communications sweep in 2022 and Epic v. Google (Mar 2023, monetary sanctions for Chat auto-delete), there are eight named modern enforcement events worth $100M+ each that all trace back to the same root cause — **routine retention beat litigation hold to the data** ([Banking Dive 2022](https://www.bankingdive.com/news/sec-cftc-fine-11-banks-18b-in-record-keeping-probe/632870/), [EDRM Epic/Google 2023](https://edrm.net/2023/04/lessons-from-googles-spoliation-sanctions-dont-dismiss-systemic-holds-or-chat/), [CourtListener Coleman 2009](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1559094/coleman-parent-holdings-v-morgan-stanley-co-inc/)).
2. **The "purge-vs-hold race" is the single most-cited failure mode** across the modern caselaw (Zubulake IV/V 2003-2004, Pension Committee 2010, Brookshire 2014, Epic/Google 2023, Albertsons FTC 2024, Pable v. CTA 2025, the Slack/N.D. Ohio 2024 trademark case). In every case, the data was technically deletable when the hold arrived; the hold-issuance system did not have a structural override on the deletion system ([Reed Smith Zubulake retrospective 2025](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102jeyn/landmark-zubulake-e-discovery-precedents-revisited-twenty-years-later/), [Lexology Pension Committee 2010](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=ce09eced-0fed-4f1e-acef-b7e4434f578e), [Texas Sup Ct Brookshire 2014](https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/supreme-court/2014/10-0846-1.html)).
3. **The Rule 37(e) intent standard makes the substrate matter more, not less.** Hoffer v. Tellone (2d Cir. Feb 2025) and Gregory v. Montana (9th Cir. Sep 2024) confirmed that "intent to deprive" — not negligence — gates the worst sanctions. That sounds defendant-friendly, but it pushes plaintiffs to argue **systemic recklessness**: a system that physically cannot honor a hold *is* intent-to-deprive evidence ([Lexology Hoffer 2025](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=d2527464-cffa-407e-be92-beffa02aea9d), [Winston & Strawn Gregory 2024](https://www.winston.com/en/insights-news/significant-sanctions-ruling-by-the-ninth-circuit-rejects-inherent-authority-and-reinforces-specific-intent-standard-for-case-ending-sanctions)).
4. **The DSAR-vs-retention compromise is universal and ugly.** NetSuite's PI Removal anonymizes the row but keeps the financial record ([Houseblend NetSuite GDPR](https://www.houseblend.io/articles/netsuite-gdpr-compliance), [Stratokey NetSuite anonymization](https://www.stratokey.com/anonymization/netsuite-anonymization)). SAP ILM does the same via "blocking" rules ([Proceed Group SAP ILM + GDPR](https://proceedgroup.com/data-management/sap-ilm-retention-management-and-blocking-for-gdpr)). Odoo's `privacy_lookup` writes a `privacy.log` and anonymizes selected fields. Every one of these layers the same hack — **field-level erasure, record-level retention** — on top of a system that wasn't designed for it. kontor's bitemporal substrate + `:db/purge`-as-recorded-commit (ADR-007) can express the same compromise *as a first-class operation*, not a wizard.
5. **DSAR cost is dominated by labor.** Gartner / DataGrail put the average manual DSAR at ~$1,524/request ([DataGrail 2026 DSAR guide](https://www.datagrail.io/blog/data-privacy/the-2026-guide-to-dsar-automation/)). Volume rose 43% from 2023→2024 ([DataGrail 2025 Trends](https://www.datagrail.io/press/datagrail-reports-worldwide-surge-in-data-privacy-requests/)). The labor goes into "where does this person's data live across our systems" — a problem that a *single-DB cohabitation* design (ADR-002) sidesteps entirely.
6. **The pricing wedge is real.** Relativity ≈ $200k/yr + per-GB; OneTrust starts at ~$500/mo but enterprise deployments are $100k+ for a mid-market full DSAR module; Logikcull is the cheapest dedicated tool ([Relativity pricing](https://www.relativity.com/pricing/), [Logikcull pricing](https://www.logikcull.com/pricing), [TrustRadius eDiscovery pricing](https://solutions.trustradius.com/buyer-blog/ediscovery-pricing/)). An SMB doing $5M/yr in revenue with ~12 DSAR/yr and no active litigation cannot justify any of these. **kontor + a thin consumer app can give SMBs a defensible-by-construction `:legal-hold` invariant + DSAR log primitive for the cost of running the kernel** — a meaningful new wedge.

## 1. Litigation-hold horror-story compendium

Eight-to-twelve documented enforcement-failure cases, grouped by the **system failure mode**, not the chronology. Each entry: case + cite + sanction size + the specific mechanism that failed.

### 1.1 Purge-during-hold (cron beat the hold)

This is the dominant failure mode. The legal hold was issued (or duty-to-preserve had attached); a routine deletion/rotation/auto-delete ran anyway; data was gone.

**Zubulake v. UBS Warburg LLC (S.D.N.Y. 2003-2004; "Zubulake IV" 220 F.R.D. 212, "Zubulake V" 229 F.R.D. 422).** The foundational case. UBS's failures (per Zubulake V):
- Failed to issue a written litigation-hold notice when duty attached.
- Failed to communicate the preservation duty to all "key players."
- Failed to suspend its standard 90-day backup-tape recycling.
- Failed to monitor compliance — multiple custodians deleted emails after the hold should have been in place.

Sanction: monetary fees, adverse-inference jury instruction; jury awarded $29.3M. ([Wikipedia Zubulake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zubulake_v._UBS_Warburg), [Reed Smith retrospective 2025](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102jeyn/landmark-zubulake-e-discovery-precedents-revisited-twenty-years-later/), [CloudNine summary](https://cloudnine.com/ediscoverydaily/case-law/ediscovery-history-a-look-back-at-zubulake/)).

**The system failure:** the *retention process* (backup tape rotation, mailbox auto-deletion) was not paused when the *legal-hold process* fired. They were independent systems with no coupling.

**Coleman (Parent) Holdings, Inc. v. Morgan Stanley & Co. (Fla. Cir. Ct. 2005; reversed on damages 20 So.3d 952 (Fla. App. 2009)).** Court found Morgan Stanley:
- Continued its 12-month automatic backup-tape deletion despite ongoing discovery obligations.
- Overwrote tapes containing relevant emails.
- Filed multiple false discovery certifications about completion of email search.

Trial-court remedy: partial default, adverse-inference instruction; jury verdict $1.45B compensatory + $850M punitive (later reversed for damages-proof reasons, but the spoliation sanction itself stood — a *liability-establishing* sanction rather than a damages award). ([Compass Lexecon case page](https://www.compasslexecon.com/cases/coleman-parent-holdings-inc-v-morgan-stanley-co-inc/), [CourtListener 2009 appeal](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1559094/coleman-parent-holdings-v-morgan-stanley-co-inc/), [FindLaw 2009](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1407656.html)).

**The system failure:** identical to Zubulake. Backup-tape auto-delete and litigation-hold lived in different administrative silos.

**Pension Committee of the Univ. of Montreal Pension Plan v. Banc of America Securities (S.D.N.Y. 2010), 685 F. Supp. 2d 456.** Judge Scheindlin's "Zubulake Revisited" opinion. Held plaintiffs were grossly negligent for failing to:
- Issue a *written* litigation-hold notice (oral was insufficient).
- Identify all key players.
- Stop email deletion / preserve records of former employees.
- Preserve backup tapes when they were the sole source of relevant ESI.

Sanction: monetary fines + adverse-inference instruction against *plaintiffs* (unusual). ([Lexology 6mo retrospective](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=ce09eced-0fed-4f1e-acef-b7e4434f578e), [Davis Wright Tremaine analysis](https://www.dwt.com/insights/2010/01/recent-decision-defines-standards-of-care-for-pres), [CloudNine Pension Committee](https://cloudnine.com/ediscoverydaily/case-law/ediscovery-case-law-pension-committee/), [Delaware Litigation Blog](https://www.delawarelitigation.com/2010/01/articles/other-court-decisions/judge-scheindlin-issues-85-page-opinion-on-e-discovery-entitled-zubulake-revisited-six-years-later/)).

The opinion *codified* the failure-mode list: not just "they failed to preserve" but the **specific operational steps** that constitute gross negligence. This list is the operating spec for what a hold-vs-retention system must guarantee. (Note: Rule 37(e) was amended in 2015 and per [9th Cir. Gregory 2024](https://www.winston.com/en/insights-news/significant-sanctions-ruling-by-the-ninth-circuit-rejects-inherent-authority-and-reinforces-specific-intent-standard-for-case-ending-sanctions) and [2d Cir. Hoffer 2025](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=d2527464-cffa-407e-be92-beffa02aea9d), gross negligence no longer suffices for the harshest 37(e)(2) sanctions — *but* it remains sufficient for lesser remedies, and the operational checklist still defines reasonable practice.)

**Epic Games, Inc. v. Google LLC (N.D. Cal. Mar 2023), No. 3:20-cv-05671-JD.** Judge Donato sanctioned Google for failing to suspend the **auto-delete of Google Chat messages** (24-hour default) when the litigation hold attached. Internal "voluntary" preservation managed by employees was insufficient. Sanction: monetary fees + permissive adverse-inference. ([EDRM Epic/Google 2023](https://edrm.net/2023/04/lessons-from-googles-spoliation-sanctions-dont-dismiss-systemic-holds-or-chat/), [ABA Antitrust Section](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/antitrust_law/resources/newsletters/epic-games-v-google-lessons/), [ACEDS post](https://aceds.org/maybe-next-time-google-should-google-legal-hold-aceds-blog/)).

**The system failure:** Google Chat's product-level auto-delete (a feature for *users*) ran independently of the corporate hold system. Google did not change the policy to default-on-preservation for employees on hold until Feb 2023 — more than two years after litigation began ([Ben Edelman analysis](https://www.benedelman.org/google-search-discovery-violations/), [PYMNTS coverage](https://www.pymnts.com/cpi-posts/antitrust-sanctions-the-duty-to-preserve-chats/)).

**Northern District of Ohio (Slack retention case, 2024)** — a trademark-infringement defendant changed its Slack retention from "indefinite" to "7 days" *after notice of litigation*, destroying several thousand potentially relevant messages. The court issued mandatory adverse-inference instructions under FRCP 37(e)(2). ([Logikcull 5 key cases](https://www.logikcull.com/blog/5-key-cases-year-spoliation-sanctions-chaos), [Everlaw text-message spoliation roundup](https://www.everlaw.com/blog/case-law/spoliation-of-text-messages-and-mobile-data-notable-court-decisions/)).

**The system failure:** A *retention-policy change* (administrative action) was indistinguishable from a *deletion action* — the system did not flag that this admin change would destroy preservable data.

### 1.2 Backup-vs-hold race

The closely related sub-mode: backup tape rotation runs on a fixed cycle; the hold scope says "preserve X"; by the time IT routes the hold to the backup admin, the tape is overwritten.

**Keir v. UnumProvident Corp. (S.D.N.Y. 2003), 2003 WL 21997747.** Plaintiff requested preservation of six days of email; IT was unaware and allowed the backup tape covering those six days to be overwritten. Court imposed remedies including an order to attempt restoration at defendant's cost. ([Total Data Migration analysis of Keir](https://totaldatamigration.com/email-retention-and-litigation-how-backup-tapes-create-issues-for-attorneys/), [Smarsh backup-tape risks](https://www.smarsh.com/blog/recent-cases-show-reliance-backup-tapes-e-discovery-compliance-costly-risky/)).

**The system failure:** the hold-tracking system had no knowledge of the backup schedule; the backup system had no knowledge of holds.

This is the **exact failure mode kontor's `:legal-hold` middleware-over-`:db/purge` prevents** — the hold lives in the same store as the data, and purge is gated by the hold.

### 1.3 Auto-deleting / ephemeral messaging

A 2020s twist on Zubulake. The "data" is ephemeral by *product design*, not by *policy*. Sanctions follow when employees on hold continue to use the ephemeral channel.

**Albertsons (FTC investigation, 2024).** The FTC's Kroger / Albertsons merger investigation surfaced that executives intentionally deleted text messages despite being on hold. The court allowed continued discovery + cross-examination about the deletions at trial ([Drug & Device Law 2025](https://www.druganddevicelawblog.com/2025/09/when-the-plaintiff-destroys-evidence-dismissal-and-sanctions-in-the-age-of-ephemeral-messaging.html), [All About Advertising Law 2024](https://www.allaboutadvertisinglaw.com/2024/03/the-ftcs-and-dojs-new-magic-act-vanished-messages-will-reappear-in-discovery.html)).

**Amazon (FTC monopoly case, 2024).** FTC accused senior Amazon executives, including Bezos, of using Signal's auto-delete during the FTC investigation. The court ordered a Rule 30(b)(6) deposition on the policy and invited further sanctions motions ([Quinn Emanuel](https://www.quinnemanuel.com/the-firm/publications/noted-with-interest-disappearing-messages-permanent-consequences-ephemeral-messaging-in-discovery/), [Wilson Sonsini ephemeral messaging paper](https://www.wsgr.com/a/web/mQzGhXFDtWfFpMYApRR7Ls/from-telegraphs-to-telegram-etc-ephemeral-messaging.pdf)).

**Pable v. Chicago Transit Authority (N.D. Ill. 2025).** Plaintiff used Signal with another employee about the litigation, then claimed messages were deleted. Court allowed adverse-inference instruction. ([Drug & Device Law 2025](https://www.druganddevicelawblog.com/2025/09/when-the-plaintiff-destroys-evidence-dismissal-and-sanctions-in-the-age-of-ephemeral-messaging.html)).

**SEC off-channel-communications sweep (2022-2025).** Not a single case but a $1.8B+ cumulative enforcement action: 16 firms in Sep 2022 + 11 more in 2023 + 12 more in early 2025. JPMorgan ($200M), Morgan Stanley ($200M), Goldman ($200M), Citi, Credit Suisse, Deutsche, UBS, Bank of America Securities all fined for failure to *preserve* WhatsApp / SMS work communications ([SEC press release Sep 2022](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2022-174), [Banking Dive 2022](https://www.bankingdive.com/news/sec-cftc-fine-11-banks-18b-in-record-keeping-probe/632870/), [K&L Gates 2023 update](https://www.klgates.com/Message-Received-SEC-Zeros-In-On-Off-Channel-Communication-2-14-2023), [Wealth Management 2024](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/regulation-compliance/sec-charges-26-firms-for-off-channel-communications-violations), [LeapXpert 2024](https://www.leapxpert.com/12-credit-rating-firms-fined-88-million-by-sec-for-off-channel-violations/)).

**The system failure:** the records-retention system was designed around official channels (corporate email, dealer-floor turret recordings). When work moved to non-official channels (WhatsApp, Signal), the retention system did not know to follow.

The kontor parallel: **content-hash-as-id (ADR-038 `:audit-doc`) lets you record the existence of an off-channel message even if you can't preserve the content** — the auditable failure is *not having a content hash*, which is a binary, defensible signal.

### 1.4 Pre-hold negligence / cascade-delete

The hold was on time; the system architecture lost the data anyway.

**Brookshire Brothers, Ltd. v. Aldridge, 438 S.W.3d 9 (Tex. 2014).** A slip-and-fall at a grocery store. Surveillance video was on a 30-day auto-overwrite. Store manager preserved an 8-minute clip; the rest was overwritten before plaintiff's lawyer subpoenaed it. Texas Supreme Court reversed the trial court's spoliation instruction, holding it required *intentional* (bad-faith) spoliation. ([Justia Brookshire 2014](https://law.justia.com/cases/texas/supreme-court/2014/10-0846-1.html), [Hunton Andrews Kurth analysis](https://www.hunton.com/insights/legal/brookshire-brothers-ltd-v-aldridge-the-texas-supreme-court-changes-the-rules-on-destroyed-evidence), [Curnutt and Hafer summary](https://curnutthafer.com/post-7/), [Texas Tech Law Review note](https://scholarship.law.tamu.edu/lawreview/vol3/iss2/10/)).

**The system failure:** the *recording system* (surveillance) had a 30-day rolling buffer; the *incident-response process* (preserve relevant evidence) did not have a "lock this entire day's footage" primitive. The store could preserve a *segment* but had no way to preserve the *containing time-window* against the next sweep.

This is the surveillance-video analogue of the kontor-`:legal-hold/scope-query` problem: the hold scope must capture "everything around the event," not "the event itself," because the surrounding context is exactly what's contested.

**Cascade-delete in accounting systems.** Less a single sanction case than a *structural* concern: many SQL-based ERPs use `ON DELETE CASCADE` for referential integrity, meaning deleting a partner deletes all the partner's invoices. ([DataCamp ON DELETE CASCADE](https://www.datacamp.com/tutorial/sql-on-delete-cascade), [Stacksync MySQL cascade risks](https://www.stacksync.com/blog/mysql-cascading-changes-and-best-practices)). When a GDPR erasure request gets processed against the *partner* row, every transaction the partner appeared on becomes inaccessible — exactly the wrong shape. The industry response is `ON DELETE RESTRICT` + anonymize-in-place ([Allen Browne MS Access cascade-to-null pattern](http://allenbrowne.com/ser-64.html), [Source Daddy restrict-delete tutorial](https://sourcedaddy.com/ms-access/defining-restrict-delete-relationship.html)), which kontor expresses naturally — datahike has no cascade-delete primitive; `:db/purge` is targeted, recorded, and the EAV model means anonymizing one entity doesn't touch the postings that reference it.

## 2. Retention-vs-erasure compromises in production

The universal industry hack: **anonymize the PII fields but keep the financial / audit row**. This is GDPR-Article-17(3)(b)-compliant (legal-obligation exception) and SOX/HGB-compliant (the financial trail is intact).

### 2.1 NetSuite "PI Removal"

The most documented enterprise implementation. Per [Houseblend NetSuite GDPR analysis](https://www.houseblend.io/articles/netsuite-gdpr-compliance) and [Stratokey NetSuite anonymization](https://www.stratokey.com/anonymization/netsuite-anonymization):

**Mechanic:**
1. Admin runs a Saved Search to identify the data-subject's records.
2. `N/piremoval` SuiteScript module overwrites specific fields (first name, last name, email, SSN) with a fixed placeholder.
3. Audit-trail entries referencing the affected record have their "Audit Trail History" value replaced with a generic message — the audit row stays, but the *content* of the change is obscured.
4. The transactional records (invoices, payments, journal entries) remain intact with the anonymized partner reference.

**Limitations the docs admit:**
- It's a *manual + scripted* operation. There's no scheduled sweep.
- Anonymization is not reversible; if you do it under hold by mistake, the hold is breached.
- Custom fields and external integrations are not covered automatically — admins must catalog every place PII landed.
- The audit trail is *anonymized*, not *deleted*, which preserves the count of changes but loses the diff content. Some regulators (Spanish AEPD, French CNIL) have questioned whether anonymized audit entries are sufficient ([Houseblend NetSuite GDPR](https://www.houseblend.io/articles/netsuite-gdpr-compliance)).

### 2.2 SAP ILM "blocking"

Per [Proceed Group SAP ILM + GDPR](https://proceedgroup.com/data-management/sap-ilm-retention-management-and-blocking-for-gdpr) and [SAP Community ILM Legal Hold series](https://blogs.sap.com/2021/09/02/ilm-legal-hold-management-part-1):

**Mechanic:**
1. ILM defines a *residence period* (active usability) and a *retention period* (must keep for regulator).
2. After residence-period end, records are "blocked" — they remain in the database but are filtered out of routine queries unless the user has a "view blocked data" authorization.
3. Blocked records can be archived after retention-period end.
4. Legal holds (transaction `ILM_LHM`) override both timers: blocked records cannot be deleted while a hold references them.

**Strengths:** the residence-vs-retention distinction is the right shape. It separates "this data should not be in routine use" from "this data must be physically preserved."

**Weaknesses (per [ASUG SAP ILM panel](https://www.asug.com/insights/asug-asks-the-authors-sap-information-lifecycle-management)):**
- Implementation effort is enormous — every record class needs ILM rules; every custom Z-table needs an ILM object.
- Blocking is enforced at the application layer; direct DB queries bypass it.
- Legal hold cancellation is manual; there's no "release on case-closed-date" automation.

### 2.3 Odoo `privacy_lookup`

Per kontor's CLAUDE.md reference, Odoo `addons/privacy_lookup/models/privacy_log.py` (LGPL-3 — readable, not liftable):

**Mechanic:** a wizard that takes a partner record, lets the admin select which fields to mask, writes a `privacy.log` row recording who/when/what, and zeroes out the chosen fields. No retention-period concept; no hold concept.

This is essentially the *minimum viable* erasure mechanism — closer to "feature checkbox for GDPR" than a real retention engine. The OCA `odoo_gdpr` module adds a DSAR portal but the underlying mechanic is unchanged.

### 2.4 What kontor's substrate enables that the others don't

**Bitemporal "as-of-(tx, valid)" answers the audit's hardest question for free.** "What did this partner's PII look like at the moment of the dispute?" requires:
- NetSuite: gone — once PI Removal runs, you cannot reconstruct.
- SAP: possible via archives, but not on the live query path.
- Odoo: gone.
- **kontor: standard `:as-of-tx (pre-purge-commit)` query, no special tooling.**

The pre-purge state lives in the commit DAG (ADR-008) until you explicitly compact branches. The purge commit itself is auditable — you can prove that *the purge happened on this date, by this user, with this `:status-history/reason`*. The *fact* of the erasure becomes part of the audit chain.

This is the design lever the existing ERPs cannot match without a complete architectural rewrite. They are *current-state systems with audit trails bolted on*. kontor is an *append-only DAG with a "current state" view* — exactly the inverse, and exactly the shape the GDPR/SOX tension wants.

**The Article-17(3)(b) legal-obligation exception, expressed as data not policy.** GDPR Art. 17(3)(b) says erasure does not apply where retention is required by EU/MS law. Every commercial implementation interprets this as a *policy document* the privacy team writes. kontor can express it as:

```
:retention-policy/code :sox-financial-7y
:retention-policy/applies-to [:transaction :posting]
:retention-policy/min-keep-millis 220752000000   ; 7y
:retention-policy/legal-basis :sox-section-103
:retention-policy/jurisdictions [#country :us]
```

A DSAR-erasure operation queries `eligible-for-purge?(entity, as-of)`. If a retention policy with `:legal-basis` set says no, the erasure fails with a structured reason — not a "the privacy team will get back to you in 6 weeks" delay. The same query helper answers the regulator's "show me your retention rules" demand auditably.

**The Finnish €856,000 Verkkokauppa fine ([EDPB Finnish SA 2024](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/national-news/2024/finnish-sa-administrative-fine-eu-856000-failing-define-storage-period_en)) is the canonical "no defined retention" enforcement.** The DPA's complaint was not "you kept too long" — it was "**you cannot tell us how long you keep**." A kernel-level `:retention-policy` registry makes this answerable.

**The 2025 EDPB Coordinated Enforcement Action** explicitly targets right-to-erasure implementation across 32 EEA DPAs ([EDPB 2025 CEF report](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-02/edpb_cef-report_2025_right-to-erasure_en.pdf)). The number of cases is going up, not down.

## 3. DSAR pain ranking (top 10)

Each with severity (P0 ship-blocker / P1 bites at scale / P2 polish) and **one-line "what kontor's substrate uniquely answers."** Severity is rated against an SMB doing real EU/CA business, not Fortune-500.

1. **"Where does this person's data live across our 14 SaaS tools?"** — P0. Cost $1,524/request manually per Gartner / DataGrail ([DataGrail 2026](https://www.datagrail.io/blog/data-privacy/the-2026-guide-to-dsar-automation/)). **kontor:** if accounting + partner + invoicing + collections all live in one datahike DB (ADR-002), this query is `(d/q '[:find ?e :in $ ?subject :where (data-subject-fragments ?e ?subject)] db subject)`. Cross-SaaS sprawl is the kernel's problem to begin with — the substrate eliminates it.
2. **30-day GDPR / 45-day CCPA clock with no system reminder.** — P0. The deadline arrives and the team is still searching. **kontor:** `:dsar-request/deadline` + `:status-transition/auto-after-millis` (ADR-041) auto-fires a T-7 / T-3 / T-0 escalation. The clock is data, not a calendar entry someone forgot.
3. **Identity verification race against the clock.** — P0. CCPA's 45-day clock starts on *receipt*, not on identity verification ([Clym CCPA 45-day timeline](https://www.clym.io/blog/ccpa-45-day-response-timeline)). A long ID-verify cycle eats the budget. **kontor:** `:dsar-request/status` machine includes `:verifying-identity` as a state; the deadline is set on `:received-at`, not on transition out of verify. Statutory math is data-bound.
4. **DSAR on partner with open AR — what do you erase?** — P1. The partner is a data subject (GDPR right to erasure) and the counterparty to receivable invoices (SOX/HGB retention). The industry compromise is anonymize-partner-PII, keep-postings, but the bookkeeping side rarely knows about the privacy side. **kontor:** `:retention-policy` query + `:db/purge` of partner PII fields, postings retained with the (now-anonymized) partner ref. Single transaction. ADR-007's purge-as-commit means the erasure is itself auditable.
5. **Schrems II and the EU-subject-in-US-cloud problem.** — P1. EU subject DSAR comes in; the data lives in an AWS us-east region; SCC requires a Transfer Impact Assessment ([Pinsent Masons Schrems II](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/guides/international-transfers-schrems-ii-gdpr), [Thales Schrems II](https://cpl.thalesgroup.com/compliance/emea/schrems-ii)). Many SMBs don't have one. **kontor:** the substrate is self-hostable; an EU-customer-facing kontor tenant can be physically EU-resident. The Schrems II analysis collapses to "where is the JVM running" — which is a deployment decision, not a regulator-debated abstraction.
6. **DSAR responses keep no audit trail of their own.** — P1. The regulator's coordinated-enforcement audit ([EDPB Right of Access 2024 CEF](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=2d636b05-e34a-4f42-b60b-6990e2984abb)) found controllers retain DSARs *inconsistently* — some keep DSAR records indefinitely (privacy violation), some keep them too briefly to prove fulfillment. **kontor:** `:dsar-request` is itself a status-history entity with bitemporal queries; the regulator can be shown "here is the entire lifecycle of every DSAR for the past 3 years" as a single datalog query.
7. **DSAR-during-hold: erasure vs preservation conflict.** — P1. Subject requests erasure; data is also under litigation hold. The preservation duty wins (per Sedona Conference principles), but the system has to *know* both states for the same record. **kontor:** the `:legal-hold` middleware fires *before* the `:retention-policy` query — a single, ordered evaluation chain. The DSAR worker calls `eligible-for-purge?` and gets `:held-by [:legal-hold/code …]` as the rejection reason. That reason can be returned to the data subject as the GDPR-Art-17(3)(e) exception.
8. **CCPA's broader "consumer" definition.** — P1. CCPA includes employees and B2B contacts (post-CPRA 2023) ([Fisher Phillips CCPA deadlines](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/summary-california-consumer-rights-business-deadlines-for-responding-to-privacy-related-requests)). Most ERPs scope "data subject" to *customer*; CCPA scopes it to *anyone whose data the business has*. **kontor:** the `:partner` model is polymorphic from ADR-033 (Person/Group with role discriminator). A DSAR-search query over `:partner` matches employees, vendors, and customers without rework.
9. **DSAR-finding documentation for legal review.** — P1. Privacy team produces 47 records across systems; lawyer wants to redact items 12, 19, 31 before delivery. Audit trail of *why those were redacted* is critical for the regulator. **kontor:** `:dsar-request/findings [edn]` rows + `:audit-doc/privilege` tagging means the privilege-redaction decision is a typed, audited operation, not a Word-doc workflow.
10. **DSAR cost-per-request economics for SMB.** — P2 (becomes P0 once volume hits ~50/yr). $1,524/request × 12 = $18k/yr in labor for a small EU-customer-facing SaaS. **kontor:** if the substrate answers the cross-system query, the labor collapses to identity-verify + legal-review. The kernel doesn't fully solve DSAR economics — but it removes the largest cost driver (search labor).

## 4. Privilege failure modes (top 5)

1. **Inadvertent production / clawback failure.** Producing party releases a privileged doc; opposing party catches it; clawback agreement may not save the privilege if "reasonable steps" weren't taken. FRE 502(b) sets a fact-intensive standard ([ABA Inadvertent Disclosure](http://apps.americanbar.org/litigation/committees/commercial/articles/fall2015-1115-inadvertent-disclosure-ediscovery-avoid-waiver-privilege.html), [Logikcull "clawback can't save"](https://www.logikcull.com/blog/a-clawback-agreement-cant-save-botched-privilege-review)). **kontor:** `:audit-doc/privilege` keyword on the doc; query helper `uri-for(doc, requesting-uid)` returns `:redacted` if user lacks role. A doc *cannot* be returned via a routine export unless the export tool overrides the privilege check explicitly — and *that override* is recorded as a status-history event.
2. **Failure to log within deadline = waiver.** Multiple courts have held that failure to timely produce a privilege log waives privilege over the withheld documents ([E-Discovery LLC waiver-by-no-log](https://www.ediscoveryllc.com/court-holds-privilege-was-waived-by-failure-to-timely-provide-a-privilege-log/), [JD Supra ACEDS](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/can-failing-to-produce-a-privilege-log-8774214/)). **kontor:** a query over `:audit-doc/privilege /= nil` within a discovery scope produces a privilege-log candidate set in milliseconds. The kernel doesn't *generate* the log description text — that's review work — but it eliminates "we couldn't find the privileged docs in time."
3. **Inadequate descriptions in the log = forced waiver.** ([Redactable privilege-log guide](https://www.redactable.com/blog/what-is-a-privilege-log-a-complete-legal-overview) cites a fact pattern where 600 of 800 logged docs lost privilege for inadequate descriptions). **kontor:** the kernel doesn't fix this — it's a review-side problem. But by giving each `:audit-doc` typed metadata (kind, author, date, recipients, subject) at creation time rather than reconstructed in review, the *raw material* for an adequate description is structured. Plumbing for a consumer-side log generator.
4. **Common-interest privilege in M&A pre-closing — the Delaware trap.** Multiple recent cases have rejected common-interest claims where buyer and seller shared due-diligence material without separate counsel actually present, or where the "common interest" was commercial rather than legal ([Finnegan Delaware common-interest case](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/articles/common-interest-privilege-did-not-shield-merger-and-acquisition-information-from-discovery-in-delaware-litigation.html), [Skadden 2023 common-interest](https://www.skadden.com/-/media/files/publications/2023/10/maintainingattorneyclientprivilegeevenwithoutanattorneyrecentapplicationofthecommon-interest-doctrin.pdf?rev=cc01e44422d644b38c79805c800f6c1e), [McGuireWoods common-interest 2023](https://www.mcguirewoods.com/client-resources/privilege-ethics/privilege-points/2023/3/common-interest-doctrine-courts-disagreements-warning-part-i/)). **kontor:** `:audit-doc/privilege :common-interest` + `:audit-doc/parties [partner-A partner-B]` + `:audit-doc/counsel-present? true` makes the *factual basis* for the privilege auditable. Not a legal opinion — but the factual record that the legal argument depends on.
5. **In-house counsel privilege.** US courts often apply a *narrower* privilege to in-house counsel (the doc must be primarily for legal advice, not business advice) than to outside counsel ([Edwards Law on accidental waiver](https://edwardslawpllc.com/accidentally-waive-attorney-client-privilege-giving-opponent-privileged-information-written-discovery-responses/), [Duane Morris on common-interest limits](https://www.duanemorris.com/articles/attorney_client_privilege_common_interest_doctrines_may_provide_less_protection_0222.html)). **kontor:** `:audit-doc/privilege :attorney-client` + `:audit-doc/counsel-type :in-house | :outside` records the distinction at attach-time. A court motion to compel can be answered with structured facts about the counsel relationship at the moment of the communication.

## 5. The integration tax

The specific cross-system failures that come from bolting GRC/eDiscovery onto a separate ERP. Each of these is a vendor-by-vendor pattern that kontor's one-DB cohabitation (ADR-002) sidesteps:

1. **The hold-issuance system and the data-retention system are different vendors.** Typical mid-market stack: OneTrust (privacy) + a separate retention-policy module in NetSuite + IT's tape-rotation cron. The hold is issued in OneTrust, the data lives in NetSuite, the deletion happens at the IT layer. **All three Zubulake-class cases are this exact failure mode.** kontor: hold + retention + data are all one datahike DB; the middleware can guarantee ordering.
2. **The DSAR system queries one cluster; the data lives in another cluster.** Salesforce + NetSuite + a custom support tool, each with its own DSAR connector. The privacy team gets results from three systems, manually merges, and produces a response. Cost: $1,524/request, mostly merge labor. kontor: one query over one DB.
3. **The privilege-tag system is the document-management system; the accounting system has its own attachments.** Outside counsel sends a memo; it gets uploaded to iManage / NetDocuments with a privilege tag; it also gets attached to an invoice line in NetSuite with no tag. The accounting-side attachment leaks privilege when the invoice goes to an auditor or vendor. **kontor's `:audit-doc/privilege` is on the same row as the accounting reference**; there is no second copy.
4. **The legal-hold release process forgets to lift the hold in the retention system.** Common SAP ILM complaint per [ASUG ILM panel](https://www.asug.com/insights/asug-asks-the-authors-sap-information-lifecycle-management): the case closes, the hold is "released" in the legal team's tracker, but `ILM_LHM` is never updated, so data continues to be over-preserved (which has its own GDPR cost — the EDPB 2025 CEF on right-to-erasure explicitly targets this). kontor: `:legal-hold/released-at` is the same row that gates `:db/purge`; release is a single fact.
5. **The audit trail's audit trail.** When the audit-trail row is anonymized to satisfy a DSAR, *who decided to anonymize it* must itself be auditable. NetSuite PI Removal replaces the audit-history value with a fixed message — the *decision* to do so is not separately recorded. kontor: the `:db/purge` is itself a commit; the commit has a `:status-history/reason`; the chain is intact.
6. **eDiscovery export from production runs as a separate batch job.** Relativity / Logikcull ingest from production via a CSV / API dump that snapshots *as of export time*. Bitemporal questions like "what did the data look like at the date of the alleged breach" cannot be answered without separate snapshots ([Relativity overview](https://www.relativity.com/data-solutions/ediscovery/)). kontor: `:as-of-tx` bitemporal queries (ADR-008) make any historical state a parameter, not an export.

## 6. SMB-vs-enterprise feature ladder

Concrete cut for what ships in Stage M kernel vs companion vs integration. Goal: an SMB (1-50 employees, no in-house legal team) can defend a litigation hold *with the kernel alone*; a mid-market shop adds a small companion for DSAR portal + intake; a Fortune-500 integrates with their existing OneTrust / SAP ILM via the substrate's read-only audit-bundle export.

### SMB-MVP (kernel, Stage M scope)

These ship in `kontor.legal-hold` / `kontor.retention` / `kontor.dsar` / `kontor.audit-doc` (privilege extension):

- `:legal-hold` entity with **hybrid scope** (eid set + datalog query). One ADR.
- `:retention-policy` entity (kernel shape; l10n seeds the defaults). One ADR.
- `:dsar-request` entity with status-machine + statutory-deadline auto-escalation. One ADR.
- `:audit-doc/privilege` keyword attribute + role-gated `uri-for` query helper. Half an ADR (extends ADR-038).
- Middleware: `:db/purge` is gated by open `:legal-hold` matching the target eid. *This is the kontor-unique structural guarantee.*
- Query helper `kontor.legal/eligible-for-purge?(eid, as-of)` returns `:ok` | `:held-by [...]` | `:retained-by [...]`.
- Query helper `kontor.legal/dsar-search(subject-id, scope-keys)` returns a flat seq of entity-refs the subject's data appears on. Kernel doesn't redact — it locates.

**What this gives an SMB:** ability to issue a hold, prove the hold blocks deletion, run a DSAR search across their entire accounting + partner data, hit the 30-day GDPR / 45-day CCPA clock with auto-escalation, tag privileged documents and refuse to render them without role. **Defensible to a regulator and to a court** without buying OneTrust ($500-$10k/mo) or Logikcull (per-matter pricing).

### Mid-market companion (Stage N or later, post-pull)

These wait for a real user story to motivate them:

- `kontor-privacy` companion: DSAR intake portal (consumer-side), identity verification via OTP/email, response packaging into a downloadable archive, redaction tooling. **UI layer, not kernel.**
- `kontor-counsel` companion: `:counsel-matter` + UTBMS-coded `:legal-invoice` for outside-counsel spend tracking.
- l10n retention seeds: `kontor-l10n-de-retention` (HGB §257 10y, GoBD audit rules), `kontor-l10n-us-retention` (SOX 7y, IRS Pub 583), `kontor-l10n-eu-gdpr-defaults` (Art-5 storage-limit defaults). **Data, not code** — just like effective-dated tax-rates (ADR-026).
- Privilege-log generator (consumer-side query renderer).

### Fortune-500 integration (deferred indefinitely)

These are existing-vendor commodity and wait for explicit demand:

- OneTrust / Vanta / DataGrail connector — kontor exposes a DSAR-finding API; the enterprise privacy platform orchestrates the cross-platform search.
- Relativity / Logikcull e-discovery export — kontor exposes a *read-only audit bundle* (postings + audit-docs + status-history + hold metadata) as a sealed branch (ADR-007 + ADR-008) the e-discovery tool ingests.
- SAP ILM / Oracle GRC bridge — for tenants migrating off SAP, the ILM rules export as `:retention-policy` rows.

### Concrete pricing wedge

| Customer profile | Today's stack | Today's annual cost | kontor-substrate cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| SMB SaaS, ~5 EU customers, ~10 DSAR/yr | Manual + lawyer hour + spreadsheet | $20-30k labor, $0 license | $0 license (kernel) + ~$5-10k consumer-app setup |
| Mid-market, ~500 EU customers, ~100 DSAR/yr | OneTrust starter + manual NetSuite searches | $30-100k OneTrust + $150k labor (~100×$1,524) | OneTrust mid-tier ($30k) replaced by `kontor-privacy` consumer + ~$20k integration; labor falls because cross-system search is gone |
| Enterprise, multi-jurisdiction | OneTrust + Relativity + SAP ILM + outside counsel | $500k-$2M | Same vendors, but kontor as the SoR means fewer cross-system reconciliation events — savings on labor not license |

The wedge is **SMB and mid-market**. Enterprise won't rip out OneTrust for kontor; but enterprise might run kontor as the *system of record* with OneTrust as the orchestration layer, which is a much smaller commercial conversation.

## 7. Open questions and concrete kontor seams

These are design calls the agent's findings inform but don't fully answer. Each maps to a likely Stage M ADR (049 / 050 / 051 / 052 placeholders).

### ADR-049 candidate: `:legal-hold` shape

- **Scope-query EDN format.** Datalog `:where` clauses are the obvious choice (kernel already evaluates them). But preservation orders are written in business-English ("all communications with Acme between 2024-Q1 and 2025-Q2") and need a translation tier. Recommendation: the EDN format *is* a datalog-`:where` clause + bindings; a consumer-side wizard helps non-Clojurists author it. The hold itself stores the canonical EDN.
- **Scope-cache refresh policy.** A hold scope-query can be evaluated lazily on every purge-check, or eagerly cached and re-evaluated on a schedule. Lazy is correct but slow; eager is fast but stale (new entities matching the scope may slip through between refreshes). Recommendation: **eager cache + lazy verification on purge attempt** — purge consults the cache for the fast path; if cache says clear, run the live scope-query once before actually purging.
- **Release-vs-resurrection.** Once a hold is released, can its scope-query be replayed retroactively? GDPR audit might want to know "what data *was* under hold during 2024." Recommendation: `:legal-hold/released-at` is a fact; the hold entity is bitemporal-queryable, so "as-of 2024-06-01" returns the hold as still-in-force.
- **Multi-hold overlap.** Two cases may legitimately both scope the same partner. Both must be released before the data can purge. Recommendation: middleware checks *any* open hold against the eid; release is per-hold.

### ADR-050 candidate: `:retention-policy` shape

- **Kernel vs l10n split (confirmed by note 17 and this note).** Kernel ships the entity shape; l10n companions ship the seeds. Mirrors ADR-026 effective-dated tax-rates.
- **Conflict-of-laws resolution.** EU subject + US-jurisdiction tenant; HGB says 10y, GDPR says no-longer-than-necessary. Which retention wins? **Floor-of-most-restrictive-keep, ceiling-of-most-restrictive-erase** is the standard analysis but not always coherent. Recommendation: kernel ships the *evaluation function*; l10n companions ship the *policy ranking*. A small `:retention-conflict-rule` entity lets `kontor-l10n-eu-gdpr-defaults` declare "GDPR ceiling overrides HGB floor for personal data; HGB floor applies to commercial data."
- **Eligibility predicate signature.** `eligible-for-purge?(entity, as-of-instant, requested-by-uid)` — the `requested-by-uid` matters for the audit trail of *who triggered the policy lookup*.
- **Partial-purge.** GDPR erasure typically targets specific *fields*, not the whole record. The retention check needs to know "you are about to purge the `:partner/email` field; is that field individually covered by a retention rule?" Recommendation: `:retention-policy/applies-to` accepts either `[:entity-type]` (whole-entity coverage) or `[:entity-type :attr]` (attribute-level coverage).

### ADR-051 candidate: `:dsar-request` shape

- **Status machine vocabulary** (uses ADR-034 facet pattern): `:received → :verifying-identity → :searching → :compiling → :awaiting-legal-review → :fulfilled` | `:rejected` | `:extended` (one extension allowed per GDPR; same for CCPA). Recommendation: codify the transitions; consumer chooses when to advance.
- **Statutory-deadline math.** GDPR = +30 days from `:received-at`; CCPA = +45 days from `:received-at`; extension = +60 (GDPR) / +45 (CCPA). The `:dsar-request/jurisdiction` keyword determines the math. Recommendation: a small `deadline-for(req)` helper, jurisdiction-keyed; deadline is *computed*, not stored, to avoid drift.
- **Findings schema.** `:dsar-request/findings [edn]` is flexible but unindexed. Recommendation: `:dsar-finding` is its own entity with `{:dsar-finding/request, :dsar-finding/entity, :dsar-finding/system, :dsar-finding/decision (#{:include :redact :exempt :legal-hold})}` — indexable, queryable, retrievable for the regulator.
- **Hold-cross-check at fulfillment.** Before any `:fulfilled` transition for an erasure DSAR, the middleware checks every finding against open holds. Recommendation: a hard invariant — a fulfilled erasure-DSAR with a finding under hold is a structural error.

### ADR-052 candidate: `:audit-doc/privilege` extension

- **Kernel keyword vocabulary.** `:attorney-client`, `:work-product`, `:common-interest`, `:joint-defense`, `:settlement-communication`, `nil` (default). Recommendation: keep this *small and closed* — l10n / consumer extensions are anti-pattern for a privilege framework that needs to be defensible across jurisdictions.
- **Role-gated URI accessor.** `kontor.audit-doc/uri-for(doc, requesting-uid)` returns the storage URI if requestor has the privilege role, `:redacted` otherwise. Recommendation: the *role check* itself is consumer-side (kontor doesn't own identity per ADR-010); the *gating mechanism* is the helper.
- **Privilege-log query helper.** `kontor.audit-doc/privilege-log(scope, as-of)` returns the privileged-doc set for a discovery scope. Recommendation: kernel ships the query; consumer renders the log.
- **Counsel-type tagging.** Add `:audit-doc/counsel-type :in-house | :outside | :external-firm` for the factual record (see § 4 item 5). Recommendation: optional attribute on the doc.

### Cross-cutting open questions

- **Should `:legal-hold` cover sealed branches or only the main branch?** Datahike sealed branches (ADR-007) are append-only; the hold question becomes "can the sealed branch be compacted out from under the hold?" Recommendation: a hold prevents *both* purge and compact.
- **DSAR-finding for partners across multiple kontor tenants.** A multi-entity intercompany setup (Showcase 4) has the same person as a vendor in entity A and a customer in entity B. A DSAR must find both. Recommendation: the search helper is `:entity`-scoped (ADR-031) but accepts `:cross-entity? true` for the cross-entity case.
- **Privilege-tag survival across `:db/purge`.** If a privileged document is referenced from an entity that purges (the entity is removed but the document remains attached elsewhere), does the privilege tag persist? Recommendation: yes — privilege is on the doc, not the reference.
- **Audit-bundle export for external e-discovery.** What does a sealed branch + `:audit-doc` URIs + `:status-history` + hold/retention metadata look like as an export format the Relativity / Logikcull tools can ingest? **This is the integration seam to the Fortune-500 eDiscovery world.** Recommendation: defer to a `kontor-ediscovery-bridge` companion; out of Stage M scope.
- **The 2025 EDPB CEF on right-to-erasure** is *coordinated enforcement* — 32 DPAs auditing simultaneously. If kontor can produce a regulator-readable export of "every DSAR-erasure decision for the past 3 years with hold/retention reasons," that's a near-decisive differentiator for EU-customer-facing SMBs. Recommendation: a `kontor.dsar/regulator-export(year-range)` query helper as part of Stage M ([EDPB 2025 CEF on erasure](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-02/edpb_cef-report_2025_right-to-erasure_en.pdf)).
